Before the correct RHS is implemented, the neural network is being trained to predict the incoming radiance at a surface point based on that point's properties (position, normal, view direction, and albedo). The LHS is computed by taking the features of the first camera-ray intersection and feeding them into the network. The network's output, which represents the predicted incoming light, is then multiplied by the surface's albedo to produce the final LHS color. However, since the RHS is just a placeholder using the full path tracer, the network is actually learning to approximate the result of a multi-bounce path trace. It's learning a complex function that maps surface properties to the final color that a full path tracer would compute, including the effects of global illumination. At the same time, the reconstruction loss is still training the albedo parameters in the BRDF of the walls, which is why the colors of the walls in both the LHS and RHS visualizations are changing during training. # Running Neural Radiosity !!! NOTE Briefly describe how you compute RHS. Include the final lhs and rhs images. The RHS serves as the "teacher" signal for the neural network. It calculates a one-bounce path tracing estimate for the scattered light at the initial camera ray intersection points. After the direct emission `E` has been calculated, for every point `x` that did not hit an emitter, the `scene.sample_brdf()` function is called. This generates a new random ray direction `wi` based on the surface material and returns a `brdf_val` that incorporates the material's color, the cosine term, and the sampling PDF. A second ray is then traced into the scene, starting from the initial hit point `x` (with an offset along the surface normal) in the new direction `wi`. If this second ray intersects another surface at a point `x'`, the neural network itself is queried using the properties of this second hit point. The network's output, which is its prediction of the light leaving `x'`, is then retrieved. The final Monte Carlo estimate for the one-bounce reflected light is the product of the `brdf_val` from the first surface and the `network_output` from the second surface. This scattered light value is then added to `E` to form the complete `RHS`. ## Rendered images ![LHS 4000 steps](images/4000lhs.png) ![RHS 4000 steps](images/4000rhs.png) # Rendering Neural Radiosity !!! NOTE Compare the speed of training and rendering between neural radiosity and path tracing (Part 1). **Depending on your code and environment, either can be faster, or they can have roughly the same speed.** Analyze, in your case, what may be contributing to the difference or similarity. After some tests, I found that for both training and final rendering, the Neural Radiosity approach was slightly slower in this specific implementation. I am using a Silicon Mac. All computation is happening on the CPU. On average, training for 4000 steps with the standard path tracer took approximately 5 minutes, while training with NeRad took about 5 minutes and 10 seconds. This is because a single training step for NeuRad is computationally heavier. It must execute the `render_nerad()` function, which computes both the `LHS` (a ray trace plus a network query) and the `RHS` (a two-bounce path trace). The standard path tracer, on the other hand, only needs to perform its single forward pass for the loss calculation. For the final, high-quality rendering, the standard path tracer took on average about 1 minute and 45 seconds, whereas the NeRad render (using the `RHS`) took approximately 2 minutes. This result is less intuitive but can be explained by the specific computations involved. The `RHS` computation is a two-path trace that finishes with a query to the neural network. The standard `render()` function, when limited to a similar path length, performs a two-path trace that includes next-event estimation (shadow rays) at each bounce. In this case, the overhead of gathering all the inputs for the neural network (positions, normals, albedos) and the cost of the network's forward pass appears to be slightly greater than the cost of the additional shadow ray calculations performed by the standard path tracer, leading to a performance difference in favor of the path tracer for final rendering.
